Jury Empanelment: Guidance on Disclosure and Excusal
Preliminary Direction to the Panel

Members of the panel, before the jury is empanelled you will be given a final opportunity to apply to be excused from serving in this trial. You should listen carefully to the information that follows, so that you may decide whether there is any proper reason why you should seek excusal.

Information Commonly Given Before Empanelment

Prior to empanelment, the panel should ordinarily be told certain matters relevant to jury service. These include the nature of the charge, the identity of the accused, the names of the principal witnesses expected to give evidence, the anticipated duration of the trial, and any further matters the court considers relevant under the Juries Act 2000.

It is generally preferable for the names of expected witnesses to be read aloud, together with the names of other persons who may be referred to during the course of the trial if recognition of those persons might create embarrassment or difficulty for a juror. Where the list is extensive, the information may more conveniently be supplied in writing.

Jurors should also be informed of the expected duration of the trial, the ordinary sitting hours of the court, and any anticipated interruption in the hearing. If the court proposes to provide additional information that may bear upon juror impartiality, that matter should ordinarily first be discussed with counsel. In an appropriate case, prospective jurors may also be asked whether they hold strong views on an issue capable of affecting their impartiality.

Disclosure of Possible Prejudicial Knowledge

Each juror must bring to the case a fully open mind and must not favour one side or the other. A difficulty may arise where a juror knows a person involved in the proceeding, or has information concerning that person, because the juror may not in fact remain impartial, or may at least appear to lean toward one side. Either circumstance may affect public confidence in the fairness of the trial.

A similar issue may arise where a juror has, or believes that he or she has, prior knowledge of the facts in dispute.

For that reason, certain identifying information must be provided concerning the case and the persons connected with it. You should consider carefully whether you recognise any of the names that are mentioned, or whether you possess information or knowledge relevant to the case. If so, you should raise that matter by applying to be excused when invited to do so.

The accused is [insert name].
The accused is charged with [insert and describe the offence(s), including the name(s) of any victim(s), if appropriate].
The following persons are expected to be called as witnesses: [insert names].
The following persons may also be referred to during the trial: [insert names of other persons whose identification may affect a juror].
[Any additional information relevant to impartiality may also be provided after prior discussion with counsel.]

If any member of the panel knows the accused, or any of the persons whose names have been mentioned, or has prior knowledge relevant to the matter, that person should disclose the issue by applying to be excused.

Trial Length and Personal Circumstances

This trial is expected to continue until [insert anticipated end date]. During that period, jurors will be required to attend court on the sitting days and at the times specified by the court, subject to any notified adjournment or interruption.

Ordinarily, before the panel is assembled, the Juries Commissioner will already have informed prospective jurors of the expected length of the trial and the court’s sitting arrangements. Prospective jurors will also generally have been given the opportunity to seek deferral on grounds such as childcare responsibilities, travel commitments, employment difficulties, or health concerns that may interfere with jury service. Many such matters are resolved administratively by the Juries Commissioner. It may therefore be expected that only a relatively small number of applications based on personal circumstances will remain for the court to determine.

If you consider that the expected duration of the trial or your personal circumstances prevent you from serving, you may seek to be excused when the opportunity is given.

When Excusal May Be Appropriate

After the relevant information has been given to the panel, the court must ask whether any member seeks to be excused from serving on the jury for the trial. Under the Juries Act 2000, a prospective juror may be excused if the court is satisfied that the person would be unable to consider the case impartially, or is otherwise unable to serve.

The mere fact that a person has had a particular life experience does not, without more, require that the person be excused. For example, it should not automatically be assumed that a person who has previously been the victim of a sexual offence is more likely than any other juror to be prejudiced in a sexual offence trial. The proper question is whether the person remains capable of deciding the case fairly and without bias.

If a substantial quantity of written material is likely to be provided to the jury during the course of the trial, the judge may consider informing jurors of that fact before inviting applications for excusal. That will allow any person who has difficulty reading to seek excusal on that basis.

Likewise, if, during the taking of the oath or affirmation, it becomes apparent that some circumstance may restrict a juror’s ability to serve, such as difficulty with English or a physical condition affecting participation, the judge may consider whether excusal is appropriate.

Form and Determination of Applications

An application for excusal may properly be made in writing, since a prospective juror may reasonably wish to keep the reasons for the application confidential. Whether such a written application should be disclosed to the defence is a matter committed to the judge’s discretion.

The accused has no absolute right to be informed of the basis upon which a prospective juror seeks excusal, nor of the reasons why such an application is refused.

Equally, there is no inflexible requirement that applications for excusal be heard and determined in public. Even so, it is generally preferable that the court’s power to excuse jurors be exercised in open court, so that the public may observe the seriousness with which jury service is regarded and the care taken by the court in deciding whether a summoned person should be excused.

Other Personal Grounds

There may be other personal reasons, not already addressed, why a person believes that he or she cannot serve on the jury in the particular case. The law recognises that excusal may be appropriate where a person cannot consider the case impartially or is otherwise unable to serve.

A prospective juror should therefore reflect carefully on whether he or she can determine the matter fairly. In one sexual offence case, a prospective juror told the judge that the matter was “too close to home,” which may have suggested an inability to treat the parties fairly. At the same time, it does not follow that every person with a similar life experience is incapable of serving impartially. Many such persons do in fact serve as jurors and are able to put aside personal experience, sympathy, or bias and discharge their duty according to the evidence and the law.

For that reason, you should think carefully before seeking excusal on the ground that you cannot act impartially, or on any other ground. Jury service is one of the most important civic responsibilities a member of the community can undertake, and the justice system depends upon citizens being willing to perform that duty.
