Jury Empanelment, Juror Impartiality, and Applications for Excusal
Introduction
For many members of the panel, jury service will be an unfamiliar responsibility. It is therefore appropriate to explain at the outset the nature of your role, the procedure that will be followed during the trial, and the legal principles that govern your task.
As the trial progresses, and again at its conclusion, I will provide you with directions on the law. You must listen to those directions carefully and apply them faithfully to the facts as you find them. Should anything I say be unclear, you may raise a question in writing and pass it to my associate for delivery to me.
The Respective Roles of Judge, Jury, and Counsel
Trial by jury is a central feature of the criminal justice system. It ensures that a person accused of a criminal offence is judged by independent and impartial members of the community according to law.
The accused in this case is alleged to have committed the offence or offences charged. The accused has pleaded not guilty. It is therefore for the prosecution to prove the charge or charges, and for you, as the jury, to determine whether that burden has been discharged.
Your role is to decide the facts solely on the basis of the evidence admitted during the trial. Once you have determined the facts, you must apply the law as I explain it to you in reaching your verdict.
My role, as trial judge, is to ensure that the proceeding is conducted fairly and according to law. I will rule on questions of law and direct you as to the legal principles you must apply. However, the verdict itself is entirely a matter for you. If I make any observation about the evidence, you are not bound to accept it. You remain the judges of the facts.
Counsel for the prosecution and counsel for the defence present their respective cases and make submissions on the evidence and the law. What counsel say is not itself evidence. You may accept an argument if you find it persuasive, but if there is any inconsistency between what counsel say about the law and what I direct you, you must follow my directions.
The Need for Impartiality
Your verdict must be based only on the evidence given in court and the law as I direct you. Sympathy, prejudice, hostility, or any emotional response toward any person involved in the trial must play no part in your deliberations.
A juror must approach the case calmly, rationally, and with an open mind. The administration of justice depends upon jurors deciding cases fairly and without favour toward either side.
Information to Be Given Before Empanelment
Before the jury is empanelled, the panel should ordinarily be informed of certain matters, including the nature of the charge, the identity of the accused, the names of the principal witnesses expected to give evidence, the anticipated duration of the trial, and any further information the court considers relevant under the applicable legislation.
It is generally desirable that the names of expected witnesses be read aloud, together with the names of other persons who may be mentioned during the trial where recognition of those names could give rise to embarrassment or difficulty for a juror. If the number of names is large, it may be more practical to provide that information in written form.
Jurors should also be told not only the expected length of the trial, but, where relevant, the usual sitting hours of the court and any anticipated interruption in the hearing. If the court proposes to provide any additional information that may affect impartiality, that course should ordinarily be discussed with counsel beforehand.
In some circumstances, it may also be proper to ask prospective jurors whether they hold strong views on a matter that might bear upon their ability to remain impartial.
Possible Prejudicial Knowledge
It is essential that every juror come to the case without prior commitment and without favouring one side or the other. Difficulty may arise where a juror knows a person involved in the proceeding, or has knowledge about that person, because the juror may not in fact be impartial, or may at least appear to others to lean toward one side. Either possibility may undermine public confidence in the fairness of the trial.
A similar concern arises where a juror has, or believes that he or she has, prior knowledge of the facts in issue.
For that reason, I must provide identifying information about the case and the people connected with it. You should consider carefully whether you recognise any of the names or whether you have prior knowledge relevant to the proceeding. If so, you should seek to be excused when invited to do so.
The accused is [insert name].
The accused is charged with [insert offence(s), including any relevant particulars].
The following persons are expected to be called as witnesses: [insert names].
The following persons may also be referred to during the trial: [insert names of other relevant persons].

If you believe that the expected length of the trial or your personal circumstances prevent you from serving, you may apply to be excused when invited to do so. In deciding whether excusal is appropriate, the court is concerned with the juror’s ability to approach the matter with an open mind and to reach a verdict solely on the evidence and the law. However, where Bob has a particularly close relationship with Ted, the court may take the view that such a connection could affect his impartiality and give rise to a concern that the trial would not be seen to be entirely fair. In those circumstances, he may be excused from serving as a juror.
Excusing Jurors
Once the relevant information has been provided to the panel, the court must ask whether any member of the panel seeks to be excused from serving on the jury for the trial.
The court may excuse a prospective juror if satisfied that the person would be unable to consider the case impartially, or is unable to serve for some other sufficient reason.
The existence of some relevant personal experience does not of itself require that a person be excused. For example, a person who has previously been the victim of an offence is not automatically incapable of serving impartially in a trial concerning similar subject matter. The proper question is whether the juror can still approach the case fairly and without bias.
If it is anticipated that the trial will involve a substantial number of written materials, it may be appropriate to alert the panel to that fact before excuses are sought, so that any person with reading difficulties may apply to be excused.
Similarly, if, during the taking of an oath or affirmation, it becomes apparent that a juror has a difficulty that may impair his or her ability to serve—such as a language difficulty or a physical condition affecting participation—the court may consider excusing that juror.
Applications for excusal may appropriately be made in writing, as prospective jurors may reasonably wish to keep the reasons for their application private. Whether any such written application is disclosed to the defence is a matter for the judge’s discretion.
There is no absolute entitlement on the part of the accused to know the grounds upon which a prospective juror seeks to be excused, or the reasons why such an application is refused. Where a prospective juror has a personal friendship or close acquaintance with the accused, that relationship may bear upon the juror’s ability to consider the case impartially. Even if actual bias cannot be established, the existence of such a connection may give rise to a reasonable concern that the trial will not be seen to be entirely fair. In such circumstances, the juror’s excusal may be required in order to preserve the integrity of the trial and to maintain public confidence in the administration of justice. Nor is there any strict requirement that applications for excusal be heard and determined in public, although it is generally preferable that the court exercise that power in open court so that the public may see the seriousness with which jury service is regarded. 
Other Reasons Affecting Service
There may also be personal reasons, not already addressed, why a person feels unable to serve on the jury in the particular case. The law recognises that excusal may be granted where a person cannot act impartially or cannot serve for some other sufficient reason.
A prospective juror should consider carefully whether he or she is able to decide the case fairly. There may be circumstances in which the subject matter of the trial is so close to a person’s own experience that impartial adjudication would be difficult. At the same time, it should not be assumed that every person with some related life experience is incapable of serving fairly. Many jurors are able to set aside personal feelings, sympathy, or bias and discharge their duty according to the evidence and the law.
Because jury service is an important civic responsibility, any application for excusal should be made only after careful reflection.
Warnings Against Improper Discussion
Because impartiality must be preserved throughout the trial, jurors should avoid any situation that might compromise their neutrality or create the appearance of bias.
You should be cautious about speaking with strangers in or around the court precincts, as you may inadvertently come into contact with someone who has an interest in the matter.
You must not discuss the case with anyone other than your fellow jurors. That prohibition extends to family members, friends, and communications through social media or similar online platforms. You may indicate that you are serving on a jury and mention general matters such as the expected duration of the trial, but you must not discuss the evidence, the issues in dispute, or any aspect of the deliberative process.
If appropriate, jurors may also be warned not to discuss the case with medical professionals or counsellors except in the most general terms necessary to describe the personal impact of jury service. Even then, the evidence, the arguments of counsel, and the views expressed by fellow jurors must remain confidential.
Jurors may discuss the case among themselves while the trial is ongoing, but only in the jury room, and even then they should keep an open mind and avoid arriving at any concluded view until all the evidence has been heard, counsel have addressed them, and the judge has directed them on the law.
Conclusion
A juror’s acquaintance with the accused, a witness, or another person connected with the proceeding may raise a legitimate question about impartiality and may justify an application to be excused. However, the decisive issue is not simply whether some relationship or prior experience exists, but whether the juror is able to approach the matter fairly, impartially, and without bias. In determining whether excusal is appropriate, the court’s central concern is the preservation of a fair trial and public confidence in the administration of justice. Particular caution is required where a prospective juror has a personal friendship or close relationship with the accused. Where a juror’s relationship creates a real risk of bias, continued service is incompatible with fairness. That is why, in this case, Bob should be excused.
