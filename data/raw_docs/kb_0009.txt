4.9 Material Expert Evidence in Mental Impairment Cases

[Stage 1 – Defence Burden]
A defence of mental impairment is one for which the defence bears the onus of proof. Because of that, the defence usually determines what expert evidence it wishes to lead and what psychiatrist it wants to rely upon. At a broad procedural level, this means the prosecution does not automatically assume responsibility for every expert witness connected with the accused. The existence of a psychiatric examination, by itself, does not always compel the prosecution to call the psychiatrist.

[Stage 2 – Continuing Obligation of the Prosecution]
Nevertheless, the prosecution’s role is not exhausted by pointing to the defence burden. The prosecution must still act fairly and ensure that evidence of real significance to the issue before the jury is not omitted. When a psychiatrist who examined the accused possesses findings that go directly to whether the accused was mentally impaired, those findings may become material evidence rather than merely optional support for the defence. At that point, the prosecution must evaluate the witness as part of the evidentiary picture the jury should have.

[Stage 3 – Consequence Where Findings Go to the Central Issue]
If the psychiatrist’s examination directly concerns the mental impairment alleged by the defence and bears materially on the contested issue, the prosecution would ordinarily be required to call the psychiatrist or otherwise place that evidence before the court. In those circumstances, the psychiatrist’s evidence is not peripheral. Thus, where the defence raises mental impairment, bears the onus of proving it, and there is a psychiatrist who has examined the accused on that issue, the prosecution would generally be expected to call that psychiatrist as a witness.
