4.9 Expert Evidence and the Prosecution’s Role

[Section One – Starting Point]
Where the defence raises the issue of mental impairment, the defence bears the onus of proving that issue. Ordinarily, the party bearing that burden decides what expert material it wishes to adduce. On that starting view, the prosecution is not simply required to call every psychiatrist who has examined the accused. A psychiatric examination alone does not automatically transform the psychiatrist into a prosecution witness.

[Section Two – Why the Starting Point May Not End the Analysis]
The analysis does not stop there, because the prosecution still has a duty to assist the court by ensuring that material evidence on a contested issue is available. Where a psychiatrist has examined the accused specifically in relation to alleged mental impairment, the psychiatrist may possess evidence that bears directly on the issue the jury must decide. If so, the prosecution cannot rely only on the fact that the defence bears the onus; the material relevance of the psychiatrist’s findings becomes the central consideration.

[Section Three – Effect of Material Relevance]
Once the psychiatrist’s examination is materially relevant to the question of mental impairment, the prosecution would ordinarily be required to call that psychiatrist or otherwise ensure the evidence is before the jury. In practical terms, if the defence raises mental impairment, bears the onus of proving it, and a psychiatrist has examined the accused about that issue, the prosecution would generally be expected to call the psychiatrist as a witness because the expert evidence goes directly to a contested matter.
