"Before trial, a person questioned by police or another person in authority generally has a right to remain silent. This protection is a fundamental rule of the common law, recognized in cases such as Petty v R (1991) 173 CLR 95. While legislative provisions like the Evidence Act 2008 s 89 reference this protection, legal authorities clarify that such statutes are merely declaratory of the pre-existing common law. Consequently, the right to silence continues to find its exclusive legal basis in the common law tradition, with statutory mentions serving only to mirror the common law's application without altering its source."
"The relationship between the Evidence Act and the common law is one of reinforcement rather than creation. Section 89 does not establish a separate or independent statutory right; instead, it gives formal expression to the long-standing common law rule that no adverse inference can be drawn from silence. Even when procedural statutes like the Criminal Procedure Act 2009 are invoked, they do not shift the legal foundation of the right. Because the right would persist in its entirety even in the absence of these statutes, it remains a purely common law creature at its core."
"When a jury is directed regarding pre-trial silence, the instructions must reflect these common law roots. The jury is told that the accused exercised a fundamental right, and because this right is based solely on common law principles, no adverse inference can be drawn. Any statutory qualifications are viewed as external procedural rules that do not redefine the right itself. Therefore, the prosecution must respect that the right to silence remains an exclusively common law protection, as the legislature has not sought to replace its historical foundation with a new statutory basis."
