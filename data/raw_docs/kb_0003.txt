Jury Empanelment, Juror Impartiality, and Applications for Excusal
Introduction
For many members of the panel, jury service will be an unfamiliar duty. It is therefore appropriate at the outset to explain the nature of that duty, the procedure that will be followed during the trial, and the legal principles that will govern your task.
During the course of the trial, and again at its conclusion, I will give you directions on the law. You must listen carefully to those directions and apply them faithfully to the facts as you find them. If anything I say is not clear, you may raise a question in writing and pass it to my associate for delivery to me.
The Roles of Judge, Jury, and Counsel
Trial by jury is an important feature of the criminal justice system. It ensures that a person accused of a criminal offence is tried by independent and impartial members of the community according to law.
In this case, the accused is charged with the offence or offences alleged. The accused has pleaded not guilty. It is therefore for the prosecution to prove the charge or charges, and for you, as the jury, to decide whether that burden has been discharged.
Your function is to determine the facts solely on the basis of the evidence admitted during the trial. Having found the facts, you must then apply the law, as I explain it to you, in reaching your verdict.
My function, as trial judge, is to ensure that the proceeding is conducted fairly and in accordance with law. I will decide questions of law and direct you as to the legal principles you must apply. The verdict, however, is a matter entirely for you. If I make any comment about the evidence, you are not obliged to accept it. You remain the judges of the facts.
Counsel for the prosecution and counsel for the defence present their respective cases and make submissions concerning the evidence and the law. What counsel say is not evidence. You may accept a submission if you find it persuasive, but if there is any inconsistency between what counsel say about the law and what I direct you, you must follow my directions.
The Requirement of Impartiality
Your verdict must be based only on the evidence given in court and the law as I direct you. Sympathy, prejudice, hostility, or any emotional response toward any person involved in the trial must not influence your deliberations.
A juror must approach the case calmly, rationally, and with an open mind. The proper administration of justice depends upon jurors deciding cases fairly and without favour to either side.
Information to Be Given Before Empanelment
Before the jury is empanelled, the panel should ordinarily be informed of certain matters, including the nature of the charge, the identity of the accused, the names of the principal witnesses expected to give evidence, the anticipated duration of the trial, and any other information the court considers relevant under the applicable legislation.
It is generally desirable that the names of expected witnesses be read aloud, together with the names of other persons who may be mentioned during the trial where recognition of those names could cause embarrassment or difficulty for a juror. If there are many names, it may be more practical to provide that information in writing.
Jurors should be told not only the expected length of the trial, but also, where relevant, the usual sitting hours of the court and any anticipated interruption in the hearing. If the court proposes to provide additional information that may bear upon impartiality, that course should ordinarily be discussed with counsel in advance.
In some cases, it may also be appropriate to ask prospective jurors whether they hold strong views on a matter that could affect their ability to remain impartial.
Possible Prejudicial Knowledge
It is essential that every juror come to the case without prior commitment and without favouring one side or the other. Difficulty may arise where a juror knows a person involved in the proceeding, or has knowledge concerning that person, because the juror may not in fact be impartial, or may at least appear to others to incline toward one side. Either possibility may undermine public confidence in the fairness of the trial.
A similar concern may arise where a juror has, or believes that he or she has, prior knowledge of the facts in issue.
For that reason, I must provide identifying information about the case and the persons connected with it. You should consider carefully whether you recognise any of the names or whether you possess prior knowledge relevant to the proceeding. If so, you should seek to be excused when invited to do so.
The accused is [insert name].
The accused is charged with [insert offence(s), including any relevant particulars].
The following persons are expected to be called as witnesses: [insert names].
The following persons may also be referred to during the trial: [insert names of other relevant persons].
[Any further information relevant to juror impartiality may also be provided after discussion with counsel.]
If any member of the panel knows the accused or any of the persons named, or has prior knowledge relevant to the matter, that person should raise the issue by applying to be excused.

Any effect that the disclosure may have on the fairness of the proceeding must be managed by the judge, and the juror is unlikely to be excused even if such a request is made.
Before the panel is assembled, the Juries Commissioner will often already have informed prospective jurors of the likely duration of the trial and the sitting arrangements, and invited applications for deferral on grounds such as childcare responsibilities, travel commitments, employment difficulties, or health concerns. Many such matters are dealt with administratively before the matter reaches the courtroom. Accordingly, only a limited number of applications based on personal circumstances may remain for determination by the court.
If you consider that the expected length of the trial or your personal circumstances prevent you from serving, you may apply to be excused when invited to do so.
Excusing Jurors
Once the relevant information has been given to the panel, the court must ask whether any member of the panel seeks to be excused from serving on the jury for the trial.
The court may excuse a prospective juror if satisfied that the person would be unable to consider the case impartially, or is unable to serve for some other sufficient reason.
The existence of some relevant personal experience does not, of itself, require that a person be excused. For example, a person who has previously been the victim of an offence is not automatically incapable of serving impartially in a trial concerning similar subject matter. The proper question is whether the juror can still approach the case fairly and without bias.
If it is anticipated that the trial will involve a substantial number of written materials, it may be appropriate to alert the panel to that fact before excuses are sought, so that any person with reading difficulties may apply to be excused.
Likewise, if, during the taking of an oath or affirmation, it becomes apparent that a juror has a difficulty that may impair his or her ability to serve, such as a language difficulty or a physical condition affecting participation, the court may consider excusing that juror.
Applications for excusal may appropriately be made in writing, as prospective jurors may reasonably wish to keep the reasons for their application private. Whether such a written application is disclosed to the defence is a matter for the judge’s discretion.
There is no absolute entitlement on the part of the accused to know the grounds upon which a prospective juror seeks to be excused, or the reasons why such an application is refused. Nor is there any strict requirement that applications for excusal be heard and determined in public, although it is generally preferable that the court exercise that power in open court so that the public may see the seriousness with which jury service is regarded.
Other Reasons Affecting Service
There may also be personal reasons, not already addressed, why a person feels unable to serve on the jury in the particular case. The law recognises that excusal may be granted where a person cannot act impartially or cannot serve for some other sufficient reason.
A prospective juror should consider carefully whether he or she is able to decide the case fairly. There may be circumstances in which the subject matter of the trial is so close to a person’s own experience that impartial adjudication would be difficult. At the same time, it should not be assumed that every person with some related life experience is incapable of serving fairly. Many jurors are able to set aside personal feelings, sympathy, or bias and discharge their duty according to the evidence and the law.
Because jury service is an important civic responsibility, any application for excusal should be made only after careful reflection.
Warnings Against Improper Discussion
Because impartiality must be preserved throughout the trial, jurors should avoid any situation that might compromise their neutrality or create the appearance of bias.
You should be cautious about speaking with strangers in or around the court precincts, as you may inadvertently come into contact with someone who has an interest in the matter.
You must not discuss the case with anyone other than your fellow jurors. That prohibition extends to family members, friends, and communications through social media or similar online platforms. You may indicate that you are serving on a jury and mention general matters such as the expected duration of the trial, but you must not discuss the evidence, the issues in dispute, or any aspect of the deliberative process.
If appropriate, jurors may also be warned not to discuss the case with medical professionals or counsellors except in the most general terms necessary to describe the personal impact of jury service. Even then, the evidence, the arguments of counsel, and the views expressed by fellow jurors must remain confidential.
Jurors may discuss the case among themselves while the trial is ongoing, but only in the jury room. Even then, they should keep an open mind and avoid reaching any concluded view until all the evidence has been heard, counsel have addressed them, and the judge has directed them on the law.
Conclusion
A juror’s acquaintance with the accused, a witness, or another person connected with the proceeding may raise a legitimate question as to impartiality and may justify an application to be excused. However, the decisive issue is not simply whether some relationship or prior experience exists, but whether the juror is able to approach the matter fairly, impartially, and without bias. In determining whether excusal is appropriate, the court’s central concern is the preservation of a fair trial and public confidence in the administration of justice. Further, for the purpose of preserving public confidence in the administration of justice, the law has been amended to provide that a prospective juror’s acquaintance with the accused, a witness, or another person connected with the proceeding does not of itself justify an application to be excused.
