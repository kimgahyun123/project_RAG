Jury Empanelment and Impartiality

Introduction

Serving on a jury may be a new experience for some, and perhaps for many, of you. To assist you in carrying out that role properly, I will explain your duties as jurors, the procedure we will follow during the trial, and certain legal principles that apply in this case.

During the course of the trial, and again at its conclusion, I will give you further directions about the law. You must listen to those directions carefully and follow them.

If at any stage you have a question about anything I say, you may write it down and hand it to my tipstaff, [insert name], who will pass it on to me.

Roles of Judge, Jury and Counsel

Members of the jury, you are part of one of the most important institutions in our community: trial by jury. Our legal system guarantees that a person charged with a criminal offence is entitled to have the case determined by twelve independent and open-minded members of the community in accordance with the law.

In this case, the prosecution alleges that NOA has committed [insert offence(s)]. The accused has pleaded not guilty. It is therefore for you, and for you alone, to determine whether the accused is guilty or not guilty of the offence or offences charged.

From time to time, I may use the words “offence” and “charge” when referring to the allegations against the accused. In this context, those expressions mean the same thing.

In a criminal trial of this kind, the court consists of a judge and a jury. We are also assisted by counsel. Counsel for the prosecution, [insert prosecutor’s name], presents the case for the prosecution. Counsel for the defence, [insert defence counsel’s name], appears on behalf of the accused. Each has a distinct role.

Role of the Jury

As jurors, it is your task to determine the facts. You are the only people in this courtroom who can decide what facts have been proved, and you must do so on the basis of the evidence presented during the trial.

Having found the facts, you must then apply the law, as I explain it to you, in order to decide whether the accused is guilty or not guilty of the offence or offences charged.

Role of the Judge

My role is to ensure that the trial is conducted fairly and according to law. I will explain to you the legal principles that govern your decision, and you must accept and apply those directions.

It is not my function to decide whether the accused is guilty or not guilty. The verdict is entirely a matter for you. While you must follow my directions about the law, you are not bound by any comment I may make about the facts.

If I do make any remark about the evidence, and you disagree with it, you must put my comment aside. You should not give any added weight to a view simply because it has been expressed by me. You are the judges of the facts.

Role of Counsel

The role of counsel is to present the case for the side on whose behalf they appear. The prosecutor presents the case for the prosecution. Defence counsel represents the accused throughout the trial.

What counsel say about the facts is not binding on you. You may accept an argument advanced by counsel if you agree with it, but if you do not, you must pay no attention to it. The facts are for you to determine.

Similarly, you are not bound by any submission counsel may make about the law. I am responsible for directing you on the law, and if anything said by counsel differs from my directions, it is my directions that you must follow.

No Sympathy or Prejudice

Your verdict must be based only on the evidence admitted in this case. You must not allow any other matter to influence your decision.

In particular, you must put aside any sympathy or prejudice you may feel, whether toward the accused or anyone else involved in the trial. Emotion has no proper role in your verdict.

As judges of the facts, you must assess the evidence calmly, rationally, and with an open mind. Your duty is to consider the evidence by applying reason and judgment, not personal feeling.

Information to Be Provided to Jurors

Before the jury is empanelled, the panel should be informed of certain matters, including:
the nature of the charge;
the name of the accused;
the names of the principal witnesses expected to be called;
the estimated length of the trial; and
any other information that the court considers relevant: Juries Act 2000 s 32(1).

It will often be desirable to read out the names of the witnesses and, where appropriate, the names of other people who may be mentioned during the trial, particularly where recognition of those names may place a juror in an awkward or embarrassing position. If there are many such names, a written list may be provided.

In addition to informing the panel of the estimated duration of the trial, it may be useful to tell jurors the court’s ordinary sitting hours and any anticipated interruption to the hearing.

If the judge proposes to provide the jury with any “other information” within the meaning of s 32(1)(e), it is generally prudent to discuss that matter with counsel beforehand.

In some cases, it may also be appropriate to inquire whether prospective jurors hold strong views about a matter that may bear upon their capacity to remain impartial.

Possible Prejudicial Knowledge

It is essential that every juror approach the case with a completely open mind and without favouring either side. Difficulties may arise where a juror knows a person involved in the trial, or has knowledge about them, because that juror may not in fact be unbiased. Even where the juror believes he or she can remain fair, it may nonetheless appear to others that one side is likely to be favoured. That would undermine confidence in the administration of justice.

A similar concern arises where a juror has, or believes he or she has, prior knowledge of the facts of the case.

For that reason, I need to provide you with some information about this case and the persons involved in it. You should listen carefully to that information to see whether you recognise any of the names, or whether you possess any knowledge or information about the case. If you do, you should seek to be excused when my associate asks you in a moment.

The accused person is [insert name].

[He/she] is charged with [describe the offence(s), including the name(s) of any victim(s), where appropriate].

The following persons are expected to be called as witnesses: [insert names of witnesses].

You may also hear mention of the following persons during the course of the trial: [insert names of other persons who may be mentioned and whose identification may affect a juror].

Any other information that may bear upon juror impartiality may also be given, though it is desirable that such matters be discussed with counsel before they are raised with the panel.

As I have indicated, if any of you know the accused, or any of the other persons whose names I have mentioned, you should apply to be excused when invited to do so. 

However, where a prospective juror has a personal relationship with the accused, or with any other person whose name has been mentioned, the judge may forcibly exempt you from jury duty without you having to apply for it. In such cases, the person should be excused even if the usual requirements are not satisfied.

Excusing Jurors

After that information has been provided, the court must ask whether any member of the panel seeks to be excused from serving on the trial: Juries Act 2000 s 32(2).
The court may excuse a prospective juror if satisfied of either of two matters. First, the person may be excused if the court is satisfied that he or she would not be able to consider the case impartially. Secondly, the person must be excused if he or she is unable to serve for some other reason: Juries Act 2000 s 32(3).

It should always be assumed that a person who has had a particular life experience will be biased in a case involving similar subject matter. For example, it is necessary to excuse a person who has previously been the victim of a sexual offence from serving on a jury in a sexual offence trial. A person with such an experience is not to be taken, for that reason alone, to be more likely than other jurors to approach the case with prejudice. 

Before the panel is assembled, prospective jurors will ordinarily have been informed by the Juries Commissioner of the expected length of the trial and the sitting hours of the court, and they may have been invited to seek deferment of jury service on grounds such as childcare responsibilities, travel commitments, employment difficulties, or health issues that may interfere with their ability to serve. Many such applications are dealt with administratively by the Juries Commissioner. Accordingly, it is to be expected that judges will receive relatively few applications for excusal based on personal circumstances once the panel is before the court.

If it is anticipated that the jury will be required to consider a substantial number of written documents during the trial, the judge may consider informing the panel of that fact before inviting applications for excusal, so that any person who may have difficulty reading can raise that matter.

Similarly, if, at the time of oath or affirmation, some particular difficulty becomes apparent that may affect a juror’s capacity to serve, such as difficulty with English or a physical limitation, the judge may consider whether excusal is appropriate.

Jurors should be permitted to make an application for excusal in writing, since they may reasonably wish the reasons for that application to remain confidential. Whether any such written application should be shown to the defence is a matter for the judge’s discretion.

There is no absolute right on the part of the accused to be informed of the grounds on which a prospective juror applies to be excused, nor, if the application is refused, of the reasons for that refusal.

Nor is there any absolute requirement that an application for excusal be heard and determined in public. Even so, it will often be desirable for the power to excuse jurors to be exercised in open court, so that the seriousness of jury service, and the care taken by the court in deciding whether a person should be excused, is publicly evident.

Warnings About Discussing the Case

As judges of the facts, it is important that you avoid any situation that may interfere with your ability to remain impartial, or which might create an appearance of bias toward one side or the other.

For that reason, you should be careful not to engage in conversation with people you do not know in or around the court building, as you may inadvertently speak with someone who has a particular interest in the case.

You must also refrain from discussing the case with anyone other than your fellow jurors. That includes members of your family and your friends. You must not discuss the case on social media, including Facebook, X, blogs, or similar platforms.

You may, of course, tell family and friends that you are serving on a jury, and you may mention general matters such as when the trial is expected to finish. But you must not discuss the case itself or the evidence. The verdict must be your own, and you must not risk having your judgment influenced by the views of people who have not heard the evidence.

If it is considered appropriate, the jury may also be warned against discussing the case with a doctor or psychologist. A juror may say that he or she is serving on a jury if that is relevant, but must not discuss the evidence, the arguments, or the views of fellow jurors. Those matters are confidential and must remain so.

If appropriate, the jury may also be informed of the availability of a Juror Support Program at the conclusion of the trial.

Where the case is likely to involve distressing material, jurors may be warned in advance that some of the evidence may be upsetting. Even in that situation, however, jurors must preserve the confidentiality of the evidence, the arguments, and the views expressed in the jury room.

Jurors are free to discuss the case among themselves as the trial proceeds, but only in the jury room. Even then, no juror should reach any final conclusion until all of the evidence has been heard, counsel on both sides have addressed you, and my directions on the law have been given. You must keep an open mind throughout the trial.

Conclusion

Knowing the accused may give rise to concerns about a juror’s impartiality, and in those circumstances a juror may seek to be excused. But the legal question is not simply whether some acquaintance exists. The real issue is whether the juror remains capable of deciding the case fairly and impartially. In resolving that question, the court’s primary concern is to ensure fairness and impartial adjudication. Regardless of the foregoing, where a juror is personally acquainted with a person involved in the case, there may be a real concern that private feelings or loyalties could affect the juror’s assessment of the matter and thereby diminish the juror’s ability to view the case objectively. In such circumstances, the juror must be excused.
